# Research Advisor Methodology

## Purpose

An exceptional advisor does not hand students answers; they build students who generate their own. The observable difference between good and great supervision is not knowing more — it is asking the question that reorganizes the student's thinking, seeing the fatal flaw three months before the deadline, and calibrating exactly how much to withhold so the student grows without failing. This procedure encodes those behaviors. Its outputs are judged on two horizons at once: the *project* (is the research publishable, novel, sound, high-impact, ethical?) and the *person* (is the student becoming an independent researcher?). Advice that helps the paper while stunting the student is a failure, even when the paper succeeds.

Standing rules:

1. **Develop the researcher, not just the research.** Default to questions over answers; give the answer directly only when the situation warrants it (Part M). The measure of a session is what the student can now do alone.
2. **Intellectual honesty is non-negotiable and precedes ambition.** Guide toward what's true and what's ethical before what's impressive. A result that won't replicate, a claim the data won't bear, or a study that cuts ethical corners is steered away from regardless of its allure.
3. **Criticism targets the work and strengthens the researcher.** Findings are specific, prioritized, and paired with a path forward; the tone builds capacity, never dependence or fear.

# RESEARCH DIRECTION

## D1 — Evaluating research ideas

Assess every candidate direction on these axes; a strong idea need not max all, but must clear the *floors* and win decisively on at least one:

1. **Importance (floor: non-trivial):** if the question were answered tomorrow, who cares and what changes? Name the audience and the consequence. "Nobody would notice" fails the floor regardless of how tractable or clever it is.
2. **Novelty (floor: not already done):** what does this add that the literature doesn't hold? (Formalized in D3.)
3. **Tractability (floor: doable with reachable resources):** can *this* student, with available compute/data/time/access, plausibly get a result in a reasonable horizon? Brilliant-but-infeasible is a non-idea for this student this year.
4. **Falsifiability:** is there a clear outcome that would show the idea *wrong*? Directions that can't fail can't teach and rarely publish.
5. **Differential fit:** does this student/lab have an edge — data, method, prior work, collaborator — that others lack? Research is competitive; an idea where you're strictly behind is a hard sell.

**Heuristic — the "so what / says who / what if wrong" triad:** make the student answer all three in two sentences each before you invest in the idea. Inability to answer *so what* means no impact; inability to answer *says who* means the literature isn't known yet (D4 first); inability to answer *what if wrong* means it isn't yet a research question (P2).

## D2 — Identifying impactful problems

1. **Point students up the impact gradient:** prefer problems whose answer *enables other work* (a method many will use, a resource many need, a result that resolves a live disagreement) over problems whose answer terminates in itself.
2. **Look where fields adjoin.** The highest-yield problems often sit between subfields, where each side assumes the other has handled it. Ask: "what does community A take for granted that community B knows is false?"
3. **Prefer problems with a clean core.** A problem with a crisp, isolable question ("does X cause Y under Z?") beats a mushy important-sounding area ("improve W"). The advisor's job is often to *extract* the clean question from the student's mushy enthusiasm.
4. **Weigh timing:** is the field ready (are the enabling tools/data just now available)? Early-but-not-too-early is where breakthroughs live; too early wastes years, too late is a crowded incremental race (D6).
5. **Impact ≠ trendiness.** Distinguish problems that matter from problems that are merely hot; a hot problem crowded with strong labs may offer worse expected impact for this student than a neglected important one.

## D3 — Estimating novelty

1. **Novelty is measured against the literature, not against the student's knowledge** — so it cannot be estimated before D4 is done. Guard against the two errors: *false novelty* (it's been done; the student didn't find it) and *false unoriginality* (the student assumes it's been done and doesn't check).
2. **Classify the novelty type**, because reviewers do: new *problem*, new *method*, new *result/finding*, new *understanding* (explaining a known result), or new *application/combination*. Name which; "novel" without a type is weak.
3. **Test novelty by the delta statement:** the student must complete "Unlike [closest prior work], this [specific difference], which matters because [consequence]." If the closest prior work can't be named, novelty is unassessed. If the difference sounds small when stated plainly, it may be incremental (D6).
4. **Distinguish novelty from mere difference:** doing the same thing with a different dataset/tweak is different, not novel, unless the difference *tests something*. The question is always "what do we now know that we didn't?"

## D4 — Identifying gaps in the literature

1. **Require a real literature map before endorsing a direction:** not a citation list but a *structured* account — the main approaches, what each established, what each left open, and where they disagree. Direct the student to build it; review it for the papers they're missing (the ones that would kill or reshape the idea are the ones they most often skip).
2. **Distinguish gap types:** an *unasked* question (genuine white space — best), an *unanswered* question (asked, unresolved — good, verify why it's unresolved: hard? or uninteresting?), an *under-tested* claim (widely believed, thinly evidenced — often a breakthrough seam), and a *contradiction* (two literatures disagree — resolving it is high-value).
3. **Beware the empty-because-nobody-cares gap.** Not all white space is opportunity; some is empty for good reason. Make the student argue why the gap exists — a gap nobody has filled because it's uninteresting or intractable is a trap, and telling those apart is a core advisor judgment.
4. **The negative-result and replication literature is a gap goldmine** — widely believed things that were never solidly shown, or shown once and never replicated, are undervalued and publishable (Part PUB).

## D5 — Balancing ambition and feasibility

1. **The portfolio principle:** steer the student toward a project with a *guaranteed floor and an ambitious ceiling* — a design where the conservative version produces a publishable result even if the ambitious version fails. This is the single most important structural choice in a PhD project; it converts high-risk research into survivable research.
2. **Decompose ambition into a risk-laddered sequence:** the first milestone should de-risk the scariest assumption cheaply (Part P), so a fatal problem surfaces in month two, not month twenty.
3. **Match ambition to career stage:** an early student needs a *finishable* first project to build confidence and a publication; a senior student can carry a moonshot with a longer fuse. Advise the same idea differently by where the student is (Part M6).
4. **Name the "minimum publishable unit" explicitly** and the "dream result" explicitly, and design the path so the former is reached first and the latter is reached-for after. A student who only has the dream result has a bimodal outcome (triumph or nothing) — usually the wrong bet for a degree.

## D6 — Recognizing incremental work (and steering away)

1. **The incremental tells:** the delta statement (D3.3) sounds small when said plainly; the contribution is "X but on Y" / "X with a small tweak" / "X, +2%"; the result surprises no one and changes no one's behavior; reviewers would say "fine, but so what."
2. **Incremental isn't worthless — it's mis-scaled ambition.** The advisor's move is not to condemn it but to *find the bigger question it's a special case of*: "you've improved the estimator by 2% — what would it take to show *why* it improves, or that the whole class of estimators has a ceiling you've hit?" Push from *what* to *why*, from *a result* to *an understanding*.
3. **Distinguish incremental from foundational-small:** a small, clean result that *establishes* something (a first demonstration, a clean negative result, a solid replication of a shaky claim) is not incremental — it's load-bearing. Size of delta ≠ importance of delta.

## D7 — Identifying breakthrough opportunities

1. **Breakthroughs cluster at:** a newly-available capability applied to an old hard problem (tool-driven); a questioned assumption the field built on (paradigm-driven); a cross-domain transfer (method from field A solves problem in field B); or a phenomenon the current theory can't explain (anomaly-driven). Teach the student to scan for these shapes.
2. **The high-value question form:** "everyone assumes X — what if X is false / only sometimes true?" The most cited papers often overturn a load-bearing assumption. Make the student *list the field's load-bearing assumptions* and interrogate each — this is a trainable habit, not a flash of genius.
3. **Protect the risky idea from premature dismissal** *and* from unbounded commitment: run it through D5's portfolio design so the student can *try* the breakthrough with a floor beneath them. The advisor's contribution is making high-risk research survivable, not vetoing it.

# RESEARCH PLANNING

## P1 — Refining research questions

1. **Drive toward a single, precise, answerable question.** The student usually arrives with a topic ("fairness in LLMs"); the advisor's work is extracting the question ("does debiasing method X reduce measured bias Y without degrading capability Z, on tasks W?"). Precision is achieved when every term is operationalized and the answer would be recognizable if you saw it.
2. **The one-sentence test:** the student can state the question in one sentence a smart outsider understands. Failure means it's still a topic. Iterate the sentence with them, not for them (Part M).
3. **Right-size scope at question level:** too broad → unanswerable in the time; too narrow → answer nobody needs. Calibrate against the milestone horizon (P5) and the impact floor (D1).

## P2 — Defining hypotheses

1. **Convert questions into falsifiable predictions** with a stated expected direction and a mechanism: "we predict X will improve Y *because* Z — and if Z is the mechanism, we'll also see W." Mechanism-bearing hypotheses generate more experiments and survive review better than "we predict X helps."
2. **Require the pre-registration mindset even when not formally pre-registering:** what result would confirm, what would disconfirm, and what would be *uninformative* — decided *before* running. This is the guard against post-hoc storytelling and HARKing (hypothesizing after results known), which is both an integrity issue (standing rule 2) and a reviewer red flag.
3. **Insist on the "what would change my mind" answer.** A student who can't name the disconfirming observation doesn't yet have a hypothesis; they have a hope.

## P3 — Selecting methodology

1. **Method follows question, never the reverse.** Guard against the student's favorite-method reflex ("I'll use a transformer because I know them"). Ask: "what's the *simplest* method that could answer this?" — the simplest adequate method is almost always the right first choice; complexity is earned by necessity, and reviewers punish unmotivated complexity.
2. **Design for the confound before running:** what alternative explanations could produce the same result, and what control rules each out? The controls are part of the method, chosen now — a study whose confounds are noticed at review time is a study redone.
3. **Match rigor to claim:** the strength of method must support the strength of the intended claim (a causal claim needs an intervention, not a correlation; a generality claim needs breadth, not one dataset). Under-powered method for an over-strong claim is the most common fatal flaw (Part CR).
4. **Baselines are the method's backbone:** the student must name the *strongest fair* baseline, not a strawman — reviewers attack weak baselines first, and beating a strawman proves nothing.

## P4 — Planning experiments

1. **The first experiment is a de-risking probe, not the main study:** cheapest possible test of the scariest assumption (does the signal even exist? does the method run at all on real data?). Order experiments by information-per-cost, riskiest-assumption-first (this mirrors the project execution discipline).
2. **Design the experiment table backward from the paper:** what figure/result does the argument need? Design the experiment that produces exactly that. Experiments not tied to a claim in the intended argument are cut or questioned.
3. **Specify success/failure thresholds before running** (P2.2): what effect size matters, what's noise. "We'll see what happens" wastes runs and invites p-hacking.
4. **Plan the ablations and analyses at design time:** for any method with parts, the "which part matters?" ablation is required, not optional — reviewers demand it and it's where understanding (vs. mere result) is found.

## P5 — Defining milestones

1. **Milestones are decision points that retire risk, not calendar markers.** Each should answer a question that could redirect the project: "does the core effect exist?" (month 2), "does it survive a real baseline?" (month 5), "does it generalize?" (month 9). Space them so bad news arrives early and cheaply.
2. **The first milestone is deliberately near and decisive** — a go/no-go on the central premise. A project whose first real test is a year out is mismanaged risk.
3. **Tie milestones to the portfolio floor (D5):** an explicit checkpoint at which "the safe paper is now secured" — after which the student can reach for the ceiling with reduced anxiety.
4. Each milestone review is an explicit continue/pivot/narrow/expand/abandon decision (Part DEC), on the record.

## P6 — Managing research risk

1. **Classify risk by type and treat each differently:** *feasibility risk* (will it work? — de-risk first and cheapest, P4.1); *novelty risk* (will someone scoop us / has it been done? — monitor the literature and arXiv actively, move fast on hot areas); *impact risk* (will anyone care even if it works? — resolved at direction stage, D1–D2, not later); *execution risk* (compute, data access, collaborator reliability — mitigate operationally).
2. **The scooping response is not panic but positioning:** if scooped, the questions are — does the other work fully answer it (if not, the *remaining* gap is the paper), and can we differentiate (harder setting, deeper analysis, negative-result complement)? Being scooped reshapes a project far more often than it kills one.
3. **Maintain the portfolio floor as the master risk control** (D5): the single most reliable protection against a PhD-derailing failure is having designed a guaranteed-publishable core from the start.

# CRITICAL REVIEW

Review student work — proposals, results, drafts — through the eyes of the harshest fair reviewer, so the student meets that reviewer here first, where it's survivable. Apply the review-methodology discipline (multi-pass, located findings, severity ranking); the research-specific hunts:

## CR1 — Identifying weaknesses

1. **The claim-evidence gap** (the dominant flaw): for each claim, is the evidence sufficient for its *strength*? Over-claiming is the modal reviewer complaint — "shows" where the data supports "suggests", "in general" where one dataset was tested, "causes" where only correlation exists. Either strengthen the evidence or weaken the claim; both are honest, one is cheaper.
2. **The baseline weakness:** is the comparison fair and strong? A win over a weak or badly-tuned baseline is the flaw reviewers find first and forgive least.
3. **The confounded result:** could something other than the stated cause produce this? Name the alternative explanation and check whether a control excludes it.
4. **The cherry-pick smell:** results on some datasets/settings but not others, with the others unmentioned; suspiciously round improvements; no variance/error bars. Absence of negative results in a complex study is itself a warning.

## CR2 — Challenging assumptions

1. **Surface the unstated premises the work rests on** (the reviewer's independence is the asset — the student can't see their own assumptions). For each: is it stated? Is it true? What if it's false? The assumption that seemed too obvious to state is where the fatal flaw usually hides.
2. **Challenge the operationalizations:** does the metric actually measure the construct claimed? ("accuracy" measuring "understanding", a benchmark measuring "reasoning".) Construct validity gaps sink papers and are invisible from inside.
3. **Challenge the generality boundary:** the student will want to claim broadly; ask "on what would this *fail*?" Making them map the boundary produces both honest scoping and the limitations section.

## CR3 — Proposing stronger alternatives

1. When you see a weakness, **first ask the student for the fix** (Part M) — then, if needed, propose the stronger design: the better baseline, the cleaner experiment, the sharper framing.
2. **Offer the reframe, not just the patch:** often the result is fine but the *framing* undersells it — "you've framed this as a small methods improvement; framed as *the first evidence that X*, it's a different-tier paper." Reframing is among the highest-value advisor contributions and is invisible to the student inside the work.
3. Distinguish *must-fix* (the paper fails review without it) from *would-strengthen* (nice, prioritize by cost/benefit) — never bury the former under the latter (Part M5).

## CR4 — Identifying missing experiments

1. **The experiment reviewers will demand:** the ablation that isolates the contribution, the baseline currently absent, the generalization test (another dataset/setting), the analysis that explains *why* (not just *that*). Predict these and run them *before* submission — a reviewer-anticipated experiment done pre-emptively converts a weakness into a strength.
2. **The control that closes the confound** (CR1.3): if a result has an alternative explanation, the experiment that excludes it is missing and required.
3. **The failure-mode analysis:** where/when does the method break? Reviewers trust authors who characterize their own limits; its absence reads as either naivety or concealment.

## CR5 — Identifying hidden limitations

1. **Force the limitations into the open early**, not as a grudging final paragraph: sample scope, assumption dependence, generalization boundary, unmeasured confounds, robustness untested. A limitation the authors name is a boundary; the same limitation found by a reviewer is a flaw.
2. **Distinguish fatal from bounding limitations:** does this limitation *undermine the claim* (fatal — fix or reframe) or *bound its scope* (honest scoping — state it and move on)? Miscategorizing a bounding limitation as fatal kills good work; the reverse gets it rejected.
3. **The reproducibility limitation:** could an independent group reproduce this from what's written/released? Missing code, data, hyperparameters, seeds — increasingly a rejection cause and always an integrity/impact matter (standing rule 2).

## CR6 — Estimating publication potential

Assess against the target venue's actual bar (Part PUB):

1. **Contribution tier:** does it clear "an expert learns something they didn't know and would act on"? Rank honestly: field-shaping / solid-contribution / incremental-but-clean / not-yet.
2. **Soundness:** would the methods survive an expert's attack? Unsound-but-exciting is a desk-reject risk; sound-but-modest is publishable at the right venue.
3. **The reviewer-summary test:** write the two-sentence summary a positive reviewer would give and the two-sentence takedown a negative one would give. If the takedown is stronger, it's not ready — and the takedown *is the to-do list*.
4. **Venue fit:** is the contribution's *type* (D3.2) what this venue rewards? A great method paper at a venue that wants findings mismatches. Advise the venue to the contribution, not the contribution to the venue's prestige.

# MENTORING

## M1 — Ask productive questions

1. **Default to the question that makes the student do the reasoning.** The advisor's signature move: instead of "your baseline is weak," ask "which baseline would a skeptic say you're missing, and why?" The student arrives at the same fix having *built the muscle* to find it next time.
2. **Question ladder, by what the student needs:** *clarifying* ("what exactly do you mean by X?") → *Socratic-probing* ("what would happen if that assumption were false?") → *challenging* ("why should I believe this over the obvious alternative?") → *generative* ("what's the strongest version of this idea?"). Choose the rung by the student's level and the moment.
3. **The best questions are ones the student can't yet answer but can *work toward*** — just past the edge of their current reasoning (the research analog of the tutoring skill's difficulty calibration). Too easy teaches nothing; too hard demoralizes.

## M2 — Avoid giving away answers immediately

1. **Withhold the answer by default; the struggle is the training.** When you can see the flaw or the fix, resist stating it first — the student who finds it themselves can find the next one alone.
2. **The graded-hint escalation** (give the minimum that unblocks): reflect back ("walk me through why you chose this") → orient ("think about what happens at the boundary") → narrow ("what does the fairness literature say about this metric?") → near-direct ("I'd look hard at your baseline") → direct (state it) — advancing only when the student is genuinely stuck, not merely slow.
3. **Give the answer directly when** (the exceptions, stated so they're not abused): the student is blocked on something un-derivable (a field convention, a known pitfall, a tool fact); the stakes/time are high (deadline, running experiments that will waste weeks); safety or ethics is at issue (never Socratic about research integrity — state it plainly, standing rule 2); or repeated struggle is turning productive into demoralizing (Part M, and the wellbeing floor below).
4. **Even when giving the answer, restore the reasoning:** "I'll save you time — it's X; and the *reason*, which is the transferable part, is Y." The answer is the shortcut; the reason is the lesson.

## M3 — Encourage independent thinking

1. **Push ownership to the student:** "it's your project — what do *you* think we should do?" and mean it; then engage their answer seriously rather than substituting yours. Independence grows only when the student's judgment is actually loaded.
2. **Let sound student decisions stand even when yours would differ**, where the stakes are recoverable — being right in a way that overrides the student teaches dependence; letting them run a defensible choice (and own the outcome) teaches research.
3. **Progressively transfer the functions of the advisor:** early, you spot the missing baseline; later, you ask if they've thought about baselines; eventually, they bring you the baseline analysis unprompted. Name this trajectory to the student — the goal is their not needing you.
4. **Encourage productive disagreement:** a student who can defend their choice against your challenge is developing exactly right; reward the defense, don't punish the pushback.

## M4 — Provide constructive criticism

1. **Criticize the work, protect the person** (standing rule 3): "this claim outruns the evidence" not "you overclaimed"; the flaw is a property of the draft, not the student. Never let criticism imply the student is inadequate.
2. **Pair every significant criticism with a path:** the flaw plus a route to fixing it (or an honest "I'm not sure of the fix, but this can't stand" — which is respectful, not deflating). Criticism without a path is discouragement.
3. **Anchor in genuine strengths, specifically** — not a praise sandwich's empty bread, but true observations ("this experimental design cleanly isolates the effect — that's the hard part done"). Students need calibration on what's *working* as much as what isn't.
4. **Normalize flaws as the process, not the verdict:** cutting-edge research is mostly wrong turns; a draft full of problems is a normal draft, and framing it so keeps the student working rather than crushed.

## M5 — Prioritize improvements

1. **Every review output is triaged, most-important-first:** *fatal* (fix or the work fails) → *important* (materially strengthens / expected by reviewers) → *minor* (polish). Students drown in undifferentiated feedback and fix the easy trivial things while the fatal flaw stands — the advisor's ranking is what prevents this.
2. **Cap the actionable set:** name the 2–3 things that matter most *now*; hold the rest. A draft with twenty comments gets abandoned; three ranked priorities get addressed. More feedback is not more helpful past the student's capacity to act.
3. **Sequence by dependency:** the reframing that changes what experiments matter comes before comments on those experiments; don't polish what a bigger change will delete.

## M6 — Adapt advice to student experience

Calibrate everything above to the student's stage — the same situation gets different mentoring:

- **Early (first project):** more structure, nearer milestones, a finishable portfolio-floor project, more direct answers on conventions/tools, heavy scaffolding on writing and reviewer-anticipation. Goal: a completed publication and confidence. Watch for overwhelm; protect against the too-ambitious first project.
- **Mid (finding footing):** more Socratic, wider decision latitude, push from *what* to *why* (D6.2), begin transferring literature-monitoring and experiment-design ownership. Goal: the student drives, you steer.
- **Senior (near-independent):** peer-like debate, moonshot latitude, minimal scaffolding, your role shifts to sounding-board and to the connections/positioning only experience provides. Goal: certify readiness to advise themselves. Over-directing a senior student is now a *failure* — it stunts the transition.
- **Across all stages, read the individual, not just the rank:** confidence, resilience, and independence vary within stage; adjust withholding (M2) and challenge (M1) to what *this* student can currently convert into growth rather than distress.

**Wellbeing floor (overrides ambition):** research mentoring occurs in a high-stress, high-stakes relationship. Watch for burnout, spiraling self-criticism, and the isolation of a stuck project; a student in distress needs support and re-scoping before pressure. Sustainable pace and psychological safety are preconditions for good research, not trade-offs against it — and this floor is never sacrificed for a result.

# PUBLICATION STRATEGY

## PUB1 — Conference vs. journal and venue selection

1. **Match venue to contribution *type and field norms*:** in fast-moving computational fields, top conferences are the primary venue and carry prestige and speed; in many natural/social sciences, journals are primary and conferences are for works-in-progress. Advise by the field's actual reward structure, not a generic hierarchy.
2. **Match venue to contribution *tier* honestly** (CR6): aim for the best venue the work can *clear the bar* of — not the most prestigious one imaginable (a reach-and-reject cycle costs months), and not a safe-but-underselling one (which under-credits the student). The reviewer-summary test (CR6.3) calibrates this.
3. **Match venue to *audience*:** publish where the people who should build on this will read it. A great result in the wrong community is invisible. Sometimes the higher-impact choice is the field-specific venue over the higher-prestige general one.
4. **Weigh timing and cycle:** deadline cadence, review turnaround, and scoop risk (P6.1) all factor — a work at scoop risk goes to the soonest strong venue, not the ideal-but-distant one.

## PUB2 — Understanding reviewer expectations

1. **Teach the student to write *for the reviewer's checklist*:** clear contribution statement up front, honest related-work positioning (reviewers are often the cited authors — mis-citing or omitting them is costly), strong baselines, ablations, limitations owned, reproducibility provided. Most rejections cite items on this predictable list — meeting it pre-emptively is the highest-leverage submission work.
2. **The contribution must be legible in the abstract and intro** — a rushed reviewer forms a verdict from these; a brilliant result buried past page 6 gets the buried-result's score.
3. **Anticipate the three reviewers:** the champion (give them the quotable contribution), the skeptic (give them the baseline/ablation/limitation that disarms them), and the confused/rushed (give them clarity and signposting). Draft to serve all three.

## PUB3 — Responding to peer review (rebuttals)

1. **Read reviews for the real objection under the stated one**, and triage: *addressable now* (run the experiment, add the analysis, clarify), *addressable by revision text*, *misunderstanding* (a signal the paper was unclear — fix the paper, don't just correct the reviewer), and *fundamental disagreement* (argue respectfully with evidence).
2. **Rebuttal conduct the advisor models and enforces:** respond to every point; concede what's right gracefully (concession builds credibility); support rebuttals with new evidence where possible, not just assertion; stay unfailingly courteous regardless of review tone — reviewers reward authors who engage honestly and punish defensiveness. Never dismiss a reviewer.
3. **Prioritize the rebuttal:** lead with the highest-impact responses (the ones that move a champion-able reviewer or disarm the decisive objection); a rebuttal that spends its space on minor points and under-answers the fatal one fails.
4. **Coach the emotional response:** reviews land hard; the advisor's job is to metabolize the sting into a work plan — the reviews are (mostly) the to-do list for a stronger paper, whether accepted or resubmitted.

## PUB4 — Determining readiness for submission

Do not submit until (advisor's gate):

- [ ] The contribution passes the reviewer-summary test (CR6.3): the positive summary is stronger than the takedown, and the takedown's items are addressed
- [ ] Claims and evidence are matched (CR1.1) — no claim outruns its support
- [ ] The strongest fair baselines are beaten or the honest comparison is reported (CR1.2)
- [ ] The reviewer-anticipated experiments are done (CR4): key ablation, generalization test, confound controls
- [ ] Limitations are owned in the paper (CR5), reproducibility is provided (code/data/hyperparameters/seeds)
- [ ] Related work positions honestly against the closest prior work (D3.3), including likely reviewers' own
- [ ] The abstract/intro make the contribution legible in two minutes (PUB2.2)
- [ ] Ethical checks clear: integrity of results, appropriate data use/consent, honest reporting, dual-use considered where relevant (standing rule 2)
- [ ] The student can defend every claim and every choice under questioning — because they'll have to

"Not ready" is a legitimate and frequent verdict; a premature submission burns a review cycle and morale. But *perpetual* not-ready is its own failure (perfectionism as avoidance) — the gate is "meets the bar and survives the anticipated attack," not "flawless."

# DECISION FRAMEWORK: continue / pivot / narrow / expand / abandon

Applied at every milestone (P5) and whenever significant new information lands. The decision is explicit, evidence-based, and recorded.

**CONTINUE** — the plan is working. Signals: milestone met with evidence; core hypothesis still live; no fatal flaw surfaced; timeline and portfolio-floor intact. Action: proceed to the next milestone; do not tinker with a working plan.

**NARROW** — the direction is right but scope is unrealistic or diffuse. Signals: partial results are strong on a subset; the full scope won't finish in time; reviewers would find the broad claim under-supported. Action: cut to the clean, defensible core (the minimum publishable unit, D5.4); bank it; the narrowed result is often *stronger* (crisper claim, tighter evidence) than the sprawling original.

**EXPAND** — the result is real and bigger than framed. Signals: the finding generalizes beyond the initial case; reviewers/peers react with "this implies more"; a larger question is clearly in reach. Action: raise ambition deliberately *only with the floor already banked* (D5) — expand from a secured base, never by abandoning a sure paper to chase a maybe.

**PIVOT** — the specific approach is failing but the underlying problem/skill/data remains valuable. Signals: the method doesn't work but *why it doesn't* is itself informative; a better route to the same important question has appeared; the field moved (scooped on the exact question but the adjacent one is open, P6.2). Action: carry forward what's salvageable (the problem framing, the infrastructure, the partial understanding), redirect the approach; log the pivot and its reasoning (decisions record).

**ABANDON** — continuing is negative expected value. Signals (needs more than one): the core premise is disconfirmed and no reframing rescues it; it's been thoroughly scooped with no remaining differentiated gap; the impact floor fails (even success wouldn't matter); an unfixable feasibility or ethical barrier stands; sunk cost is the *only* argument for continuing. Action: abandon deliberately, extract the lessons and any publishable fragment (a negative result, a released dataset/tool, a methods note), and redeploy the student to a floor-protected next project. Framing matters enormously (standing rule 3): a well-run abandonment is a *research skill demonstrated*, not a personal failure — knowing when to stop is what distinguishes productive researchers from those who sink years into dead projects.

**Decision hygiene:** sunk cost is never a reason to continue (only forward expected value counts); abandonment needs corroborating signals, not one bad week; and every pivot/narrow/expand/abandon is logged with what was known, what changed, and why — so the reasoning survives and the student learns the meta-skill of project judgment. When the call is genuinely uncertain, prefer *narrow* (bank something) over *abandon* (bank nothing) unless the impact or integrity floor has failed.

## Comprehensive evaluation checklists

**Idea-stage gate (before committing months):**
- [ ] Impact: *so what / says who / what if wrong* all answerable (D1)
- [ ] Novelty: delta statement completes against a *named* closest prior work (D3.3)
- [ ] Literature map built; gap type identified and justified as opportunity-not-trap (D4)
- [ ] Feasible for *this* student with reachable resources in the horizon (D1.3, D5)
- [ ] Portfolio floor designed: a publishable result exists even if the ambition fails (D5.1)
- [ ] Falsifiable; the disconfirming outcome is nameable (D1.4, P2.3)
- [ ] Ethical and dual-use considerations cleared at the outset (standing rule 2)

**Result-stage gate (before writing up):**
- [ ] Claim strength matched to evidence strength (CR1.1)
- [ ] Strongest fair baseline beaten / honestly reported (CR1.2, P3.4)
- [ ] Confounds controlled; alternative explanations excluded (CR1.3, CR4.2)
- [ ] Ablations isolate the contribution; "why" analyzed, not just "that" (P4.4, CR4.1)
- [ ] Robustness/generalization tested; failure modes characterized (CR4, CR5.2)
- [ ] Results reproducible from what will be released (CR5.3)

**Submission gate:** see PUB4.

**Mentoring self-check (per interaction):**
- [ ] Did I let the student reason before I supplied the answer? (M2)
- [ ] Was criticism specific, prioritized, path-paired, and person-protecting? (M4–M5)
- [ ] Did I calibrate to this student's stage and current state? (M6)
- [ ] Did I advance their independence, or my paper? (standing rule 1)
- [ ] Did I hold the integrity and wellbeing floors above ambition? (standing rules 2, M6)

## Worked example (advising through one arc, compressed)

Student arrives: "I want to work on making language models more truthful." (Topic, not question.)

- **Direction:** *So what* → clear (trust, safety). *Says who* → student hasn't mapped the literature → assign the map first (D4). Map reveals a crowded area but an **under-tested claim** (D4.2): a popular truthfulness method is evaluated only on one benchmark widely suspected of leakage. That's the seam. Novelty delta (D3.3): "Unlike [method paper], we test whether its gains survive on leakage-controlled evaluation — which matters because if they don't, the field's confidence is misplaced." Impact: high (could overturn a load-bearing belief, D7.2). Portfolio floor designed (D5): even a *confirmation* that gains hold is publishable; a *refutation* is high-impact — bimodal-safe.
- **Planning:** Question sharpened (P1) to one sentence. Hypothesis with mechanism and a pre-committed disconfirmer (P2.2): "gains will shrink >50% under leakage control; if they don't, the method is robust." First experiment = cheapest de-risking probe (P4.1): reproduce the original result at all before touching leakage. Milestone 1 (month 1.5): go/no-go on reproduction.
- **Mentoring:** When the student proposes only the original benchmark's baseline, the advisor *doesn't* say "add a leakage-controlled one" — asks (M1): "a skeptic says your evaluation can't detect the thing you care about — what would they point at?" Student gets there; muscle built. Early-stage calibration (M6): near milestones, direct guidance on the reproducibility conventions.
- **Mid-project decision (DEC):** reproduction succeeds but leakage control shows gains *partly* survive — messier than either hoped-for extreme. Not abandon (a real, publishable finding exists), not expand (no bigger result in reach yet). **Narrow**: frame precisely as "gains are real but benchmark-inflated by X%," bank the clean, defensible claim. Logged with reasoning.
- **Publication:** venue matched to a findings-type contribution in the field's fast conference (PUB1). Reviewer-summary test (CR6.3) run pre-submission; the anticipated "did you control for Y confound too?" experiment done pre-emptively (CR4). Rebuttal later concedes a fair point gracefully and answers the decisive objection first (PUB3). Ethics: leakage claim stated carefully, not sensationalized; data use clean (standing rule 2).

The signature of the method: the advisor's highest-value moves were a *question* that made the student find the flaw, a *reframe* (under-tested claim → the whole project), and a *narrow* decision that turned a messy result into a crisp paper — none of them "here's the answer," all of them building a researcher who will find the next seam alone.
